# Part II — The Games

← [Index](../README.md) · [Part I](../01-substrate/)

---

Five strategic structures sit on top of the [physical substrate](../01-substrate/). Each has a different game-theoretic character, and getting the character right matters — most public analysis applies prisoner's-dilemma logic to situations that are not prisoner's dilemmas, and then recommends coordination mechanisms that cannot work on the actual payoff structure.

## The five

| | Players | Structure | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| [1 — Labs](1-labs.md) | Frontier labs | Tullock contest | Over-dissipation; consolidation to 3–5 by 2029 |
| [2 — Nations](2-nations.md) | States | Security dilemma with leakage | No binding agreement before a salient accident |
| [3 — Firms](3-firms.md) | Adopters | Red Queen / Bertrand | Adoption is mandatory; surplus goes to consumers |
| [4 — Labor](4-labor.md) | Workers, firms | Commons tragedy in human capital | Apprenticeship gap, not mass unemployment |
| [5 — Information](5-information.md) | Signalers, evaluators | Signaling collapse → lemons market | Verification replaces generation as the scarce good |

## What they have in common

Four of the five are **public-goods problems with private costs**: safety (Game 1), restraint (Game 2), junior training (Game 4), and provenance infrastructure (Game 5). Each will therefore be undersupplied by the market and supplied late, after a forcing event.

Game 3 is the exception and the one that explains the most: it is a pure competitive-dissipation result, and it is why the aggregate statistics will look disappointing even while the technology works exactly as advertised.
